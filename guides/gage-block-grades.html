Gage Block Grades: 00, 0, AS-1, AS-2, and B

A grade is the tolerance the blocks were made and certified to. It is not a different stack. A grade 00 one-inch block and an AS-2 one-inch block are both nominally 1 inch. The grade says how far the true length is allowed to sit from that nominal size, and how much the length is allowed to vary across the face.

The names in use

ASME B89.1.9 is the US gage block standard. It names four grades: 00, 0, AS-1, and AS-2. Grade 00 is the tightest. AS-2 is the widest. The length limit gets wider as the block gets longer, so you read the row for that nominal length. The B89.1.9 length tolerance applies across the measuring face, not only at the center reference point. That is a real difference from older specs that toleranced the center and then added a separate variation.

Grade B is not one of those four names. Federal Specification GGG-G-15A listed AAA, AA, A+, A, and B. The later federal spec GGG-G-15C replaced that list with grades 0.5, 1, 2, and 3. Shops that still say "B blocks" are using the older commercial name for a working set. Do not confuse it with ASME B89.3.7 grade B, which is a grade of granite surface plate.

ISO 3650 is the metric gage block standard. Its grades are K, 0, 1, and 2. Grade K is the calibration grade. The 1998 edition of ISO 3650 withdrew grade 00. An ISO grade 0 is not a synonym for ASME grade 0, and ISO grade 2 is not a synonym for AS-2. Use the grade system printed on the certificate.

Length tolerance through 1 inch

Public comparison charts of ASME B89.1.9 agree on the length tolerance, in millionths of an inch, for the short rows below. One millionth of an inch is 0.000001 in. Variation in length and flatness are separate limits in the standard and are not in this table. Longer blocks have wider length limits. The certificate that came with the set is the row that applies to those blocks.

What to buy for the job

A stack that lives in a setup, and that you wring and take apart all day, is usually AS-2. Inspection and a check of other gages usually want grade 0 or AS-1, with a certificate. Grade 00 belongs where it is the master for other blocks, not in a machine vise. A workshop grade, including an old grade B set, is the wrong master for checking a micrometer if the job calls for an inspection grade.

Worked example

The 1.000 in block in an 81-piece set is one block. The calculator does not care which grade it is. Open 1.000 in on the 81-piece set and the result is the 1.000 block. If the certificate says that block is 1.000004 in, the measured length is the certificate value. The nominal stack is still 1.000 in, and the grade only tells you whether a departure of 0.000004 in was allowed.

On the "through 1 inch" row, ±0.000004 in is inside the grade 0 chart limit of ±0.000006 in and outside the grade 00 chart limit of ±0.000003 in. That comparison is the chart, not a calibration. The lab's reported deviation and its uncertainty are the numbers you use on a job.

FAQ

Which ASME grade is the tightest?

ASME B89.1.9 names grades 00, 0, AS-1, and AS-2. Grade 00 is the tightest of those four. AS-2 is the widest. The tolerance also depends on the nominal length, so a long grade 00 block is not held to the same inch limit as a 1 inch grade 00 block.

Is grade B an ASME gage block grade?

No. ASME B89.1.9 does not use grade B. Grade B is a name from the older Federal Specification GGG-G-15A, which listed AAA, AA, A+, A, and B. It is also not the same thing as ASME B89.3.7 grade B, which is a surface-plate grade.

Does the calculator apply the grade tolerance?

No. The calculator adds nominal block sizes. The grade is the tolerance band those blocks were made to. The calibration certificate is the measured deviation of each block you actually have.
