Ceramic vs Steel vs Carbide Gage Blocks

The calculator treats a block as its nominal size. The material is what decides how that size moves when the room is not 20 °C, and what happens when the block is dropped, left damp, or wrung all day. These notes use the coefficients in NIST Monograph 180, Table 3.2, at 20 °C. A set's certificate overrides the table.

Materialα at 20 °CWhat that means in the shop
Steel, blocks under 25 mm11.5 × 10⁻⁶ / °CClosest of the three to most steel parts. Rusts. Needs a light film in the case and a wipe before the next wring.
Zirconia ceramic9.2 × 10⁻⁶ / °CCloser to steel than carbide is. Does not rust. Hard. Can chip if it is struck.
Chrome carbide8.4 × 10⁻⁶ / °CBetween zirconia and tungsten carbide. NIST lists it separately from tungsten carbide.
Tungsten carbide4.5 × 10⁻⁶ / °CAbout a third of steel, which is how NIST describes it. Wears well. Heavy. The expansion difference against a steel part is the large one.

A long steel block is not 11.5. NIST lists a 500 mm steel gage block at 10.6 × 10⁻⁶ / °C. ISO 3650 specifies steel as (11.5 ± 1.0) × 10⁻⁶ per K. The temperature calculator uses 11.5, 9.2, 8.4, and 4.5 as presets and lets you type the certificate value.

Do not mix them in one stack

Wringing a steel block to a carbide block can make a joint. It does not make one coefficient. Off 20 °C each block grows by its own α. The nominal sum is then the wrong length.

Worked difference, steel part against tungsten carbide blocks, 100 mm, 5 °C above 20 °C:

ΔL = 100 × (11.5 − 4.5)×10⁻⁶ × 5 = 0.0035 mm

The part is 3.5 µm longer than the carbide stack, relative to how they compared at 20 °C. The same sum with steel blocks against a steel part at the same 11.5 coefficient is zero to first order. Both grew. The comparison did not. A part alloy that is not 11.5 still needs its own coefficient. Type both into the temperature page.

Which one to keep at the bench

Steel is the ordinary choice when the work is steel and someone will actually oil the set. Carbide earns its place as a working set that is wrung constantly, if you will correct for temperature or you work close to 20 °C. Zirconia is the corrosion-resistant choice when the expansion still needs to stay nearer steel than carbide does. None of the three is a reason to skip the certificate. Grade and material are different choices. The grades page is the tolerance. This page is the metal, the ceramic, and the carbide.

The stack for a nominal 1.000 in block is the same list in the calculator whichever material the block is. Open 1.000 in. The temperature change is not in that list.

FAQ

Which material expands like a steel part?

Steel blocks. The nominal coefficient is 11.5 × 10⁻⁶ per °C, close to many steel workpieces, so the difference can be small when both are at the same temperature. Carbide at 4.5 × 10⁻⁶ and zirconia at 9.2 × 10⁻⁶ do not match steel as closely. Those three numbers are the NIST Monograph 180 table values at 20 °C.

Can I wring a steel block to a carbide block?

They can wring. You should not treat that stack as one material when the room is off 20 °C. Each block grows by its own coefficient. Build the stack from one material unless you are prepared to correct each block.

Do ceramic and carbide rust?

Not the way steel does. Steel needs a rust-preventive film in the case. Ceramic and carbide still need clean faces. Carbide is heavy and can chip if it is dropped. Ceramic can chip if it is struck. Steel dents and rusts more readily than it chips.
