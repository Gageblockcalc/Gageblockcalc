Gage block sets

Buy the rectangular set the calculator already knows. Square blocks are a laboratory form and will not match these stacks. Steel is the usual shop material. Ceramic and carbide resist corrosion. They expand at a different rate than steel, so do not mix materials in one stack when the room is off 68 °F (20 °C). See ceramic, steel, and carbide and the temperature correction.

Match the grade to the job. A working set for setups is often ASME B89.1.9 grade AS-2. Inspection and calibration want a tighter grade, often grade 0 or AS-1, plus a calibration certificate. A laboratory grade is wasted in a vise. A workshop grade is the wrong master for checking other gages. Some inch catalogs call an 81-piece set an 83-piece set because two 0.050 inch wear blocks are in the box. Count the measuring blocks, not the marketing number.

  • 81-piece inch. The set to compare first for inch work. It steps by 0.0001 inch: the ten-thousandths series, the thousandths series, the 0.050 inch series, and the long blocks. Open the 81-piece guide and try 1.2345 in.
  • 36-piece inch. A smaller shop set: 0.050, a short 0.0001 inch series, 0.101 through 0.109, 0.110 through 0.190, 0.100 through 0.500, and 1 through 3 inch. It cannot make every 0.0001 inch target an 81-piece set can. Open the 36-piece guide and try 0.7505 in.
  • 28-piece inch. A thin set, about 0.020 to 0.240 inch, with a 0.02005 inch block so it can make a half-tenth step. It does not replace an 81-piece set. Open the 28-piece guide and try 0.12345 in.
  • 112-piece metric. 0.001 mm and 0.01 mm series around 1 mm, 0.5 mm steps through 24.5 mm, and long blocks at 25, 50, 75, and 100 mm. Open the 112-piece guide and try 41.125 mm.

Sine bars

A sine bar sets an angle from a gage block stack. Stack height is the bar length times the sine of the angle. The length that matters is the center distance of the rolls, not the overall length of the steel. This calculator uses 5 inch, 10 inch, 100 mm, and 200 mm bars, which are the sizes most shops buy.

Look for a stated center distance, hardened and ground rolls, and a note of how far that distance is allowed to be from nominal. A bar sold as “about 5 inches” with no tolerance is a layout tool. A sine plate is the same idea with a wider top and clamps so the work stays put. The stack still comes from the gage block set.

Micrometers

Start with a 0 to 1 inch or 0 to 25 mm outside micrometer. Look for carbide faces, a lock, a ratchet or friction stop, and a stated accuracy. A mechanical outside micrometer usually reads 0.0001 inch or 0.001 mm. Sizes above 1 inch should include a setting standard. A digital micrometer is easier to read and still needs the same checks.

A zero check only shows that the anvils meet. Check several sizes so the spindle stops at different thimble positions. On a 0 to 1 inch micrometer, common sizes are 0.105, 0.210, and 0.315 inch.

Calipers

A 0 to 6 inch or 0 to 150 mm caliper is the everyday tool. Digitals often read 0.0005 inch or 0.01 mm. The accuracy is usually several times that resolution, so a caliper does not replace a micrometer. Look for jaws that close flat, a lock, and a depth rod only if you will use the depth rod. Inside, outside, and depth are three different measurements. Check the outside jaws on a gage block. A slide that feels gritty will not repeat.

Indicators and dial test indicators

A plunge dial indicator measures along its stem. A 0.001 inch graduation covers a longer travel. A 0.0005 or 0.0001 inch graduation covers a short travel and is for comparison, not for measuring a one-inch part in one move. Read the range and the graduation together.

A dial test indicator has a lever stylus. Use it to tram a vise, sweep a bore, or set work on a sine bar. If the stylus is not tangent to the surface, cosine error shows up in the reading. Match the back to the holder you already have: lug, flat back, or dovetail. A jeweled movement lasts longer on a machine that vibrates. A test indicator is not a long-range dial indicator, and a dial indicator is a poor tram tool.

Height gages

Vernier, dial, or digital. For layout, 0.001 inch is a normal resolution. An inspection height gage often carries a test indicator instead of a scriber. Look for a fine-adjust knob and a base wide enough that the column does not rock. The scriber is for marking. Measuring wants a finger or an indicator. The plate under the gage matters more than the name on the column.

Granite surface plates

ASME B89.3.7 grades are AA (laboratory), A (inspection), and B (toolroom). A 12 by 18 inch plate is a bench start. A larger plate needs a stand that supports it at the points the maker specifies. Look for a stated grade and a flatness number, plus a cover. A listing that only says “granite” is not a grade. Do not use the plate as an anvil or a welding table. A height gage on a bench that is not flat only repeats the bench.

Parallels

Buy matched pairs, hardened and ground, with a stated parallelism. A set from 1/8 inch up through about 1-1/2 inch covers most milling setups. Soft parallels dent and stop matching. They are setup tools, not length standards. Do not substitute a parallel for a gage block when the tolerance is in tenths.

1-2-3 blocks

A matched pair of 1 by 2 by 3 inch blocks, hardened, with tapped holes, is for clamping and spacing in a setup. Look for a stated squareness. They are not gage blocks. The holes and the size are convenient, and the tolerance is looser than a gage block grade. A pair that is not matched will rock a vise jaw. Do not wring them into a stack and treat the result as a calibrated length.

Edge finders

A mechanical edge finder usually has a 0.200 inch tip. Run it at a moderate spindle speed, touch off until the tip kicks, and remember the reading is to the tip radius, not to the finished edge. An electronic edge finder lights or beeps and is simpler if you rarely indicate. A bent or dirty tip repeats the wrong place. For tenths, sweep with an indicator instead.

Gage block accessories and wringing

Holders, clamps, and scribers let a stack stand in as a height reference. The holder has to support the stack without tilting it. A cocked holder throws away the grade you paid for. Wear blocks, often two 0.050 inch blocks on an inch set or two 1 mm blocks on a metric set, and often carbide, take the contact so the set does not. A kit sold for wringing is usually a stone, oil, and a case. Clean, flat faces wring without a gadget.

Inspection care: stones and oil

A serrated gage block stone knocks down a light burr so a block wrings again. It does not restore a worn block, and grit left on the stone laps the face and changes the size. Wipe the stone. Use it only when a block will not wring and you can see a nick.

After you handle steel blocks, wipe on a light gage-block oil or a rust-preventive film, then wipe that film off before the next wring. A thick oil film adds length and stops a good wring. Store steel in the case, not loose in a drawer. Fingerprints rust. Ceramic and carbide do not rust the way steel does. Keep the certificate with the set, and do not treat a stone as a substitute for calibration.

FAQ

Which gage block set should I compare first?

For inch work, start with a rectangular 81-piece set. It steps by 0.0001 inch across the sizes a shop uses most. A 36-piece set is smaller and cannot make every 0.0001 inch target. A 28-piece thin set covers about 0.020 to 0.240 inch, including a 0.00005 inch step. For metric work, a 112-piece set has 0.5 mm steps through 24.5 mm and long blocks at 25, 50, 75, and 100 mm. An 88-piece set steps the long blocks by 10 mm.

What grade of gage block should a shop buy?

Match the grade to the job. A working set used for setups is often ASME B89.1.9 grade AS-2. Inspection and calibration want a tighter grade, often grade 0 or AS-1, and a calibration certificate. A laboratory grade is wasted on a stack that lives in a machine vise. A workshop grade is the wrong master for checking other gages.

Can I try a stack before I buy the set?

Yes. The calculator is free and does not ask you to sign in. Open the 81-piece, 36-piece, 28-piece, 112-piece, or 88-piece guide and read the blocks. Buy the set that can build the sizes you actually use.

What should I look for on a micrometer or a caliper?

Read the stated accuracy and resolution, not only the brand. A 0 to 1 inch outside micrometer should include a standard for larger sizes and a ratchet or friction stop. Check it on gage blocks away from zero, such as 0.105, 0.210, and 0.315 inch. A zero check only shows that the anvils meet. A caliper is coarser than a micrometer.

Do I need a granite surface plate with a height gage?

Yes, if the height gage is how you inspect or lay out. The plate is the reference. Look for a stated grade: ASME B89.3.7 grade B for a toolroom and grade A for inspection. A height gage on a bench that is not flat only repeats the bench.
